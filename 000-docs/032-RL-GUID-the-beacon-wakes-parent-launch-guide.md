# The Beacon Wakes Parent Launch Guide

> Draft product and content contract. It is not yet a published guide, validated teaching method, or learning-outcome claim.

## Purpose

Help a parent introduce typing practice without turning it into punishment, surveillance, or another homework conflict. The parent does not need teaching experience. The child should understand that they are being invited into an adventure, retain meaningful choice, and be able to stop without losing access or status.

The guide is part of the permanent free trust layer from Intent Solutions. The useful core is available without an email address. Parent updates and the Friday letter are a separate optional adult choice.

## Free package

| Asset | Parent time | Job |
|---|---:|---|
| One-page quick start | 2 minutes | explain the invitation, setup, first session, and stopping rule |
| Five-minute parent lesson | 5 minutes | demonstrate supportive language, accuracy-before-speed, and calm correction |
| `Light the Beacon Together` | under 10 minutes | let parent and child begin one mission together, then transfer control |
| Seven-day family rhythm | optional | offer several short sessions without streaks, countdowns, or guilt |
| Resistance and accessibility card | as needed | help the parent adapt or stop safely when the experience is not working |

The package may appear as a web page, printable PDF, captioned short video, and in-app first-session option. The content contract remains the same across formats.

## One-page quick start

### 1. Invite

Suggested parent language:

> I found a game where your typing wakes up a broken world. Want to try the first mission with me for a few minutes? You can stop if it is not fun.

Do not lead with grades, future jobs, required minutes, comparison with another child, or a promise that the game will make the child fast.

### 2. Give real choices

Before play, the child chooses whether to try, who touches the keyboard first, sound level, readable text settings, and whether the parent stays nearby. As the product gains multiple interest worlds, world selection becomes another choice. Do not advertise a choice that the current build does not implement.

### 3. Set up for comfort

- Place the keyboard and screen at a comfortable height.
- Turn on the untimed path if pace creates pressure.
- Use the largest readable type and reduce motion when useful.
- Let the child choose one or two hands according to the supported access path.
- Remove unrelated notifications before the mission.

### 4. Play together briefly

The parent types one short transmission and makes one ordinary mistake on purpose. The parent models a calm correction: `That key did not work. I will look, adjust, and try again.` The child then takes control for the remainder of the mission unless they ask for help.

### 5. End positively

Stop at the first natural mission ending, or earlier if the child asks. Ask one neutral question: `What part felt most interesting, confusing, or annoying?` Do not demand another round to protect a streak or improve a score.

## Five-minute parent lesson

### Minute 1: The role

The parent is a co-pilot, not a judge. Their job is to make the environment calm, notice frustration, help with settings, and protect the child's ability to choose and stop.

### Minute 2: What to notice

Notice whether the child can find the requested keys, recover from an error, understand what changed in the world, and remain physically comfortable. Speed is not the first target.

### Minute 3: What to say

Use specific, low-pressure observations:

- `You caught that mistake and fixed it.`
- `That route changed when the message went through.`
- `Want the next one untimed, or are you finished for today?`

Avoid labels and comparisons:

- `You are a natural.`
- `Your sister was faster.`
- `You only need five more minutes.`
- `Do not break your streak.`

### Minute 4: When to help

Offer help after the child asks, repeatedly cannot locate a control, or shows clear frustration. Prefer changing the setting or explaining one control over taking the keyboard away. If text, motion, sound, hand position, attention, or keyboard layout is creating a barrier, adapt the experience rather than blaming effort.

### Minute 5: When to stop

Stop when the child asks, reports discomfort, becomes increasingly upset, or can no longer understand the task. A calm early ending is a successful use of the stopping rule. The product must never make purchased access, rewards, or status depend on continuing through distress.

## `Light the Beacon Together` first session

| Time | Experience | Product behavior |
|---:|---|---|
| 0:00 to 1:00 | Parent previews the mission and settings | adult-facing setup is outside active child play |
| 1:00 to 2:00 | Parent offers the invitation | no countdown, checkout, or learning claim |
| 2:00 to 4:00 | Parent demonstrates one short input and calm correction | mistake feedback is specific, readable, and non-punitive |
| 4:00 to 8:00 | Child controls the mission | typing causes an immediate visible world change; untimed route remains available |
| 8:00 to 10:00 | Child finishes or stops | both completion and stopping receive a calm ending; no streak pressure |

The parent-facing setup cannot be labeled an age-verification or identity gate unless it actually provides one. Any external link, newsletter, license import, or future checkout requires an explicit adult transition after active child play.

## Optional seven-day family rhythm

This is a choice, not a compliance program. The initial draft rhythm is three short play opportunities across seven days, with a fourth optional replay or route change. The exact frequency and duration require qualified learning review and family usability testing before they become public recommendations.

- Day 1: play the first mission together.
- Day 3: child replays or continues independently.
- Day 5: child chooses a route, setting, or challenge variation that actually exists.
- Day 7: child chooses replay, continue, show someone the restored world, or stop the experiment.

There is no punishment for missed days, no streak rescue, and no parent dashboard designed to monitor compliance.

## If the child says no

The parent accepts the answer. They may ask once what made the invitation unappealing, record the response privately if participating in an approved research protocol, and offer a different time or interest framing later. They should not hide a lesson inside play, threaten loss of another activity, or use a shipped package as emotional leverage.

Common responses:

| Child response | Parent action |
|---|---|
| `Typing is school.` | acknowledge it, show the world-changing mechanic briefly, and allow a no |
| `I am bad at it.` | switch to untimed play, model a mistake, and avoid speed language |
| `This looks babyish.` | do not argue; capture the design feedback and stop |
| `My hands hurt.` | stop immediately and review physical setup before another session |
| `I cannot read or follow this.` | change supported text, sound, motion, input, or assistance settings; do not diagnose |

## Data and communication boundary

- The guide is accessible without signup.
- The game sends no child identity, interests, responses, performance, or error history to the web page or Twenty.
- A parent may separately request product updates or the Friday letter through explicit adult consent.
- Newsletter refusal does not change guide access, game access, pricing, support, or treatment.
- Research notes require a separate approved protocol, de-identification rules, retention period, and parent consent.
- Marketing analytics must not reconstruct a child profile from gameplay or handoff context.

## Validation before publication

The guide needs qualified learning, accessibility, child-safety, privacy, and plain-language review. Parent usability sessions must test whether a parent can:

1. explain the invitation and stopping rule without coaching;
2. locate untimed, sound, motion, and readable-text controls;
3. avoid speed pressure and sibling comparison;
4. distinguish adult newsletter consent from child play;
5. complete the first-session handoff without sending child data;
6. describe what the product currently teaches without overstating it.

Test with different caregiver relationships, technical confidence levels, household languages, child ages, accessibility needs, and keyboard layouts. Record limitations rather than generalizing from a convenient sample.
