## 2026-09-10 world-first naming correction

An independent critique identified a useful miss in the prior naming round: children do not choose a product because its name explains keyboard literacy. They choose a world, character, toy, or game they want to enter. The name may be arbitrary before launch if the experience gives it meaning. This moves the strategy away from mechanism-led names and toward a distinctive entertainment identity that can contain sports, robots, art, animals, space, games, music, electronics, and science.

`Dabble` is the clearest expression of the product thesis:

```text
Try worlds. Build skills. Find your thing.

They explore. They discover. They build.
```

It also has a strong semantic relationship with Intent Solutions: children explore broadly, then develop intent about what they want to pursue. That relationship should influence the brand narrative. It does not make the exact word legally or commercially usable.

### Revised naming brief

The next naming round should:

1. name a world, character, toy, or invitation rather than education or keyboard mechanics;
2. feel desirable to children ages 9 to 14 before a parent explains the learning value;
3. support many interest worlds without making one subject the default;
4. work as a game title, mascot system, physical box line, executable, and community identity;
5. be coined or sufficiently distinctive to survive search and legal review;
6. avoid direct overlap with children's education, STEM kits, typing games, sports technology, game platforms, and subscription boxes;
7. let Intent Solutions appear as the trusted maker without forcing `Intent` into the child-facing name.

Current state: the strategic thesis improved, but the name remains open. `Ninewire` and `Mapwire` are results from the earlier Grok criteria, not approved winners under this revised world-first brief.

### Universal computer-fluency naming correction

The owner clarified that the product is not a coding or robotics course. Everybody needs to type and operate a computer, while coding, robots, sports, music, art, animals, games, science, and stories are optional interest worlds that make practice worth entering.

This clarification changes the name gate:

1. the master name must not imply coding, robotics, hardware, STEM-only learning, one gender, or one culture;
2. the child should hear a world, character, movement, or invitation, not a school subject;
3. typing remains the universal control mechanic, but it does not have to appear in the master name;
4. individual worlds and products may use descriptive subtitles, such as a signal-rescue, sports-data, music, or wildlife mission;
5. the name must be short, pronounceable, and testable across the first international markets without depending on an American idiom;
6. the parent promise is broad computer fluency, useful screen time, confidence, and discovery, not an early coding career.

This makes `Tinkdab` less compelling than it appeared in the prior screen. The `Tink` prefix can pre-classify the experience as maker, robotics, or STEM content and is also close to the crowded Tinker and Tinkercad territory. `Tinkdab` is therefore demoted from provisional challenger to retained research input. It may be tested for comparison, but it is not the presumptive master brand.

The owner confirmed that Intent Solutions remains the master company brand. The child-facing name is an endorsed product brand, not a new corporate umbrella. The working architecture is now:

```text
Intent Solutions
  -> child-facing product name, endorsed as "by Intent Solutions"
     -> typing and computer fluency as the control system
     -> multiple child-selected interest worlds
     -> optional physical projects and kits after validated milestones
```

Intent Solutions should be prominent on parent, purchase, privacy, support, and trust surfaces. The child-facing product name, characters, and world should lead gameplay and packaging. This preserves one accountable company identity without forcing a corporate-sounding name into the child's experience.

### Mavis Beacon nostalgia without brand appropriation

The owner proposed a generational story that parents who remember Mavis Beacon would recognize. The emotional strategy is strong, but the public product cannot be framed as `Return of Mavis`, a new Mavis story, or an official successor without a license. `MAVIS BEACON` remains an active registered mark for educational software, and current versions continue to be offered commercially.

The approved creative territory for exploration is an original beacon motif:

```text
Parents learned to type with a guide.
Their children use typing to guide entire worlds.

The old signal went quiet.
A new generation has to relight it.
Every key changes what happens next.
```

This supports a parent-facing nostalgia bridge while preserving an original child-facing universe. The beacon may be a story object, destination, mystery, or recurring signal, but not a Mavis character, likeness, logo, interface copy, packaging style, or implied continuation. A possible story pattern is `PRODUCT: The Lost Beacon` or a first mission to relight a world beacon. This is a narrative pattern, not an approved title.

Parent-facing campaign territory may use original language such as `Typing is back. It grew up.` or `You learned to type in a classroom. They will use it to command worlds.` Any direct historical reference to Mavis Beacon in commercial copy requires legal review for accurate nominative use and must not imply affiliation, endorsement, or succession.
